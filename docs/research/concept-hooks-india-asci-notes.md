**Methodology (from the preview's methodology page)**
- Online survey of 5,000 consumers in 18 states, run with YouGov, using quota sampling.
- 51% of respondents were male and 49% female, aged 18–60.
- City-tier split: about 30% Tier 1, about 30% Tier 2 and about 40% Tier 3.
- **Every respondent was from an SEC A household**, the top socio-economic band, both salaried and non-salaried.
- **Caveat:** this is an affluent, online sample. It does not represent all non-metro consumers.
- Tier definitions: Tier 2 is the next set of cities after the metros, rounding out the top 20 cities. Tier 3 is the smaller cities, towns and semi-urban centres beyond them.

**Key findings (paraphrased)**
- **Research-driven buyers who need visual proof.** The report says non-metro shoppers want to see a product working before they buy. Demonstrations and real use matter more to them than discounts or brand recall.
  - 37% rely on YouTube reviews as their main way to discover products.
  - 32% find new products through social media.
  - 35% of Tier 2–3 consumers use e-commerce sites as a research tool before buying.
  - The report says YouTube reviews in Tier 3 have moved from a way to confirm a choice to the starting point of discovery.
- **Creators over celebrities.**
  - About 22–23% trust influencer or creator recommendations as a key decision signal. The preview's data page says 23%; its overview and closing pages say 22–23%.
  - Only about 3% say celebrity endorsements influence them.
  - The report reads this as a clear move away from advertising built on aspiration and star faces.
- **Trust comes from people, not from the brand's own claims.**
  - Word of mouth influences 22% of Tier 2–3 buyers on the data page. The "Looking Ahead" page says 24–25%, so the preview is inconsistent here.
  - 43% of Tier 3 consumers check the brand's official website before buying. The report uses this to argue against the idea that small-town buyers act on impulse.
  - 32% of Tier 2–3 buyers (31–33% in the conclusion) factor in how customer service treats them **before** they buy.
  - 23% are swayed by eco-friendly or sustainability claims, but the report says this works mainly when peers back the claim up.
- **WhatsApp is the backbone.** About 90% use WhatsApp (the conclusion gives 84–89%). The report describes it as communication tool, discovery channel and commerce enabler in one, across Tier 2 and Tier 3.
- **Festivals and discount tracking.**
  - 52% of Tier 2 consumers actively track sale days and discount cycles before buying.
  - In Tier 3, festivals are described as stronger buying triggers than promotional calendars. No percentage is given.
  - 67% of Tier 2–3 consumers use UPI as their main payment method.
- **Other platforms and channels.**
  - 54% prefer JioHotstar for content in Tier 2–3.
  - 24% of Tier 3 consumers prefer vernacular OTT services such as Zee5 and SonyLIV.
  - 46% of Tier 3 consumers use Telegram.
  - 46% use music streaming apps.
  - In-game advertising draws a response from 55% of Tier 2 and 52% of Tier 3 consumers.
  - Quick commerce is used by 36% in Tier 3, food delivery by 44% and in-home services by about 22%.
- **Emotional cues are secondary.**
  - 18% want customised or bespoke offerings.
  - 13% respond to status cues.
  - 12% respond to nostalgia-led messaging.
  - 12% respond to smart-tech or AI features.
  - Only 4% respond to pop-culture cues.
  - The report's summary: these buyers have aspirations, but they put relevance and usefulness ahead of hype.

**Uncertain / caveats**
- These figures are from the free preview. The gated full report may contain more breakdowns, such as by tier, age or category.
- The preview gives slightly different figures for word of mouth, WhatsApp and creator trust on different pages (see above).
- No margin of error or fieldwork dates are published.
- A separate Rukam Gen Z report (2026) also exists. **I did not use it in place of this report.**

  - About a third prefer products with cultural value during festivals.
  - 53% of millennials and 47% of Gen Z plan healthier festive snacks.
  - 73% of younger consumers choose sugar-free sweets.

**Methodology (per Agency Reporter)**
- Online quantitative survey in January 2025: 13,000 respondents aged 15–59 in 9 Asian countries, including 2,000 in India (Delhi, Mumbai and Bangalore only, so **metro-only**).
- Qualitative work: home visits in May 2025 with 12 participants aged 20–29 and 50–59, plus 11 expert interviews.
- Hakuhodo describes the result as more than 10,000 India-specific data points.

**Key findings (paraphrased)**
- **Friendship is gaining ground next to family.**
  - 42% of Indians name relationships at home as a major source of stress, the highest of the markets surveyed.
  - 61% get satisfaction from dependable friendships, about 1.2× the ASEAN average.
  - 49% ask friends for advice on big life decisions.
- **Relationships are taking on a friendship mindset.**
  - The study says Indians are applying a friendship style to parent–child and couple relationships.
  - 30% of couples want friendship within their romantic partnership.
  - 48% see love and marriage as separate things, about 2.3× the rate in China.
- **More focus on oneself.** 17% would rather spend on themselves than save for their children, about 3.4× the rate in China.
- **Warning against clichés.** In the Campaign India piece, Hakuhodo India COO Jayanto Banerjee says marketers should tell stories that honestly reflect this shift towards friendship **without falling back on clichés**. He suggests showing emotional conversations between friends and loosening family hierarchies on screen. The article adds that shallow "buddy culture" portrayals risk making real relationships look trivial, and that emotional pull has to come from real life rather than reused templates. Banerjee also says hyperlocal touches belong in execution details, not in the core idea of a campaign.

## 4. ASCI (Advertising Standards Council of India): current Code and guidelines relevant to short video

**Pages read (originals)**
- Code: https://www.ascionline.in/the-asci-code/ (page metadata dateModified **2025-11-21**)
- Guidelines: https://www.ascionline.in/the-asci-code-guidelines/ (page metadata dateModified **2026-11-02**)
- I downloaded both pages and read the full text.
- Neither page shows a visible "last updated" line, except for Chapter III, which shows "Last updated: April 29, 2024".
- The dates below are the Board of Governors sign-off dates printed under each section.
- A combined PDF is linked from the Code page: https://www.ascionline.in/wp-content/uploads/guidelines/ASCI_Codes_Guidelines_Book.pdf (I did not read it; its date is unknown).

**Status:** ASCI is a voluntary self-regulatory body, not a government regulator. Even so, the Cable TV Networks Rules (Rule 7(9)) bar TV ads that breach the ASCI Code, and ASCI's decisions are widely followed on digital.

### (a) Misleading claims and substantiation: Code Chapter I ("Truthful & honest representation")
- **Version:** Chapter I is signed off **10 July 2025**, the latest change to this chapter. The visible addition is clause 8, on ad disclosures on media companies' social handles.
- **Re-check by:** 2026-07-10, **OVERDUE**. In practice, check the live page.
- Key points, paraphrased:
  1. Any factual claim must be provable, and the advertiser must hand over the evidence when ASCI asks.
  2. If an ad says it rests on independent research, it must name the source and date.
  3. An ad may not refer to a person or firm without permission if that gives an unfair edge or ridicules them.
  4. An ad must not mislead through implication, omission, ambiguity or exaggeration, in words or visuals.
  5. An ad must not exploit consumers' trust or inexperience, and must not over-promise to the point of widespread disappointment. This covers:
     - "free" offers, which must disclose any cost other than delivery;
     - "buy one get one" offers, where the price must not be inflated;
     - "up to" and "from as low as" claims;
     - health or body-insecurity claims;
     - investment and contest ads.
  6. Obvious, clearly humorous exaggeration is allowed.
  7. Occasional, unintended failures to deliver may be excused.
  8. Ads on media companies' social handles must carry a label at the start, such as Advertisement, Ad, Sponsored, Collaboration, Partnership, Free Gift or a platform tag.

**Related guideline: disclaimers in ads** (signed off **13 July 2023**; re-check by 2024-07-13, **OVERDUE**). This matters a lot for short video.
- A disclaimer may clarify a claim but must not contradict it or try to correct a misleading claim.
- "T&C apply" must say where the terms can be found.
- A disclaimer must be in the same language as the claim; in a bilingual ad, the dominant language.
- If the claim is spoken in the voiceover, the disclaimer must appear on screen at the same time.
- Minimum text height: 26 px on 1080-line video, 57 px on 2160-line video.
- At most one disclaimer per frame. Keep it to two lines, held on screen for at least 4 seconds per line.
- Longer disclaimers: hold at 5 words per second, plus 2 seconds (for 9 words or fewer) or 3 seconds (for 10 or more).
- Spoken disclaimers: no faster than 6 syllables per second, at normal volume.
- The guideline advises simplifying the headline claim rather than piling on disclaimers.

### (b) Comparative advertising and disparagement: Code Chapter IV ("Fair in competition")
- **Version:** signed off **21 April 2012**; no later date is shown.
- **Re-check by:** check the live page each year, because the one-year rule from 2012 is long past.
- Key points, paraphrased:
  - Comparisons, including ones that name a competitor, are allowed if:
    - it is clear what is being compared;
    - the subject of comparison is not chosen to create an artificial advantage;
    - the facts can be proven;
    - neither product is misrepresented;
    - the ad does not unfairly run down, attack or discredit a rival's product or advertising, openly or by implication. This is the disparagement rule.
  - No unjustified use of another firm's name or initials, and no free-riding on its trademark or campaign goodwill.
  - No copying of another advertiser's layout, copy, slogans, visuals, music or sound effects in a way that looks like plagiarism. Complaints about ads first run abroad are accepted only within 12 months of first circulation.
- The disclaimers guideline adds that the basis of a comparison must appear next to the claim, in at least 25% of the claim's font size.

### (c) Harmful gender stereotypes
- **Version:** "Guidelines on harmful gender stereotypes", signed off **8 June 2022**. They interpret Chapter III and grew out of ASCI's GenderNext study with Futurebrands (Oct 2021).
- **Re-check by:** 2023-06-08, **OVERDUE**; the text is unchanged on the live page as of 2026-11-02.
- Key points, paraphrased:
  - Ads may show people in traditional roles, but must not suggest those roles belong only to one gender, are the only option for it, or are never done by another gender.
  - Children's ads must not imply that a toy, activity or career is wrong for a particular gender.
  - Ads must not tie happiness to an idealised body type.
  - Ads must not mock people who don't fit gender norms, or anyone's sexual orientation or gender identity, even as a joke.
  - Ads must not show one person (typically the woman) solely responsible for chores while others relax, or show her as overly grateful for help.
  - Ads must not show someone failing at a task because of their gender.
  - Ads must not suggest that height, skin colour or weight stops someone succeeding in love or work.
  - Ads must not sexually objectify anyone in ways unrelated to the product.
  - Ads must not normalise domination, harassment, stalking or eve-teasing.
  - Humour does not excuse a harmful stereotype.
  - Stereotypes may be shown in order to challenge them.

### (d) Influencer advertising in digital media
- **Version:** "Guidelines for influencer advertising in digital media". First released **27 May 2021** and applicable to posts from **14 June 2021** (per ASCI's 2021 press release, reported by Legal500 and others). Addendum I is dated **15 Jul 2021**; Addendum II is dated **6 Mar 2025**, and the page sign-off is 6 Mar 2025.
- **Re-check by:** 2026-03-06, **OVERDUE**.
- **Who must disclose:** any post where the advertiser and influencer have a "material connection", which includes:
  - money;
  - free products, even unsolicited ones;
  - discounts, gifts, trips or barters;
  - awards;
  - family or employment ties.
- Disclosure is required even if the review is honest. No label is needed if the creator simply bought the product and likes it.
- **Placement:**
  - The label must be upfront and hard to miss: not only in the bio, not at the end, not behind "more", and not buried among hashtags.
  - On video with no caption, the label must be superimposed on the video.
  - **Videos of 15 seconds or less:** the label stays for at least 3 seconds.
  - **Over 15 seconds and under 2 minutes:** the label stays for one third of the length.
  - **2 minutes or longer:** the label stays for the whole section about the brand.
  - Live streams: announce at the start and end.
  - Audio: announce at the start, at the end and around breaks.
- **Allowed labels:** Advertisement, Ad, Sponsored, Collaboration, Partnership, Employee, Free gift, Affiliate, or the platform's disclosure tool. The label may be in English or in the language of the ad.
- **Virtual influencers:** these are defined as computer-generated human-like avatars. They must also tell people upfront that they are not real humans. **This rule has been in the guideline since 2021.**
- **The requested "2023+ virtual influencer / AI disclosure update" was not found as a 2023 change.** The AI-related update is the separate September 2026 synthetic-content guideline in (e) below. That guideline makes labels mandatory for synthetic influencers and ambassadors.
- **Responsibility** sits with both advertiser and influencer. The advertiser must ask for edits or removal where needed, and influencers should check that the brand can back up its claims.
- **Addendum I (2021):** if someone argues there was no material connection, ASCI needs a signed declaration from the advertiser, or the influencer's proof of purchase.
- **Addendum II (2025):** influencers who give health/nutrition or BFSI advice must hold relevant qualifications and show them prominently at the start. Examples:
  - SEBI registration number for investment advice;
  - IRDAI licence, CA or CS for other financial advice;
  - medical, nursing, nutritionist or dietician credentials for health advice.
  Influencers without such qualifications may share only general, non-technical information.

**Related: celebrities in advertising** (signed off **13 July 2023**; re-check by 2024-07-13, **OVERDUE**).
- A "celebrity" is anyone paid ₹40 lakh or more a year for ads, **or with 500,000 or more followers on any one social handle**. Large creators therefore count.
- The celebrity must do due diligence on the ad's claims and give ASCI a signed confirmation. ASCI's Endorser Due Diligence service is optional.

### (e) AI-generated content and deepfakes
- **Version:** "Guidelines for Responsible Labelling of Synthetically Generated Content in Advertising".
  - The ASCI page sign-off date is **17 Sep 2026**.
  - Khaitan & Co's briefing (12 Oct 2026) and MediaNews4U (29 Sep 2026) say it was **released 29 Sep 2026**, after a May 2026 draft.
  - It **comes into force 3 months after publication**. Khaitan calculates this as **29 Dec 2026**; one search snippet suggested 1 Jan 2027, which I did not verify.
  - **Uncertain:** the exact effective date.
- **Re-check by:** 2027-09-29, and also confirm the effective date after 29 Dec 2026.
- **URLs read:**
  - ASCI guidelines page (original): https://www.ascionline.in/the-asci-code-guidelines/
  - Khaitan & Co briefing: https://www.khaitanco.com/sites/default/files/2026-10/Ergo_ASCI%20Guidelines%20on%20SGC%20Labelling_12%2010%202026.pdf
  - MediaNews4U: https://www.medianews4u.com/asci-issues-guidelines-for-labelling-ai-generated-content-in-advertising/
- **Definition:** synthetically generated content (SGC) is audio or video made or materially altered by computer so that it looks real, showing a person or event in a way people would take as genuine.
- **Approach:** the test is the effect on consumers, not the technology used.
- **Prohibited even with a label:**
  - fake endorsements or testimonials, including AI showing a real person approving a product they never used;
  - AI visuals that exaggerate results, such as an unreal before/after weight-loss body;
  - non-existent places or facilities shown as real;
  - deepfakes, a person's likeness used without consent, or unlicensed copyrighted work.
- **Label is mandatory when:**
  - the ad uses synthetic influencers or ambassadors;
  - it uses a cloned real face or voice for personalised messages, **even with consent**;
  - it shows fabricated events or settings that affect how the product is understood;
  - it shows a product that does not exist yet, such as a 3D render of an unbuilt project;
  - it uses synthetic sound that is central to the product's claims, such as headset audio;
  - an AI assistant gives a sponsored product recommendation, which must say "Sponsored by [Brand]".
- **No label is needed for:**
  - routine edits such as colour, lighting, noise or blemish clean-up;
  - decorative backgrounds, jingles and ambient sound;
  - obviously fantastical effects;
  - AI-written copy;
  - accessibility uses such as subtitles, captions and translations.
- **Suggested label wording:** "Audio/Video created using AI" or "Audio/Video enhanced using AI", the platform's own labels, or other accurate wording.
- **A label is not a safe harbour.** The full Code still applies.
- Khaitan notes that this sits alongside the IT Rules 2021 as amended in **February 2026**, which require *intermediaries* (platforms) to label all synthetic content.

### (f) Dark patterns (false urgency etc.)
**ASCI: "Guidelines for online deceptive design patterns in advertising ('Dark Patterns')"**
- **Version:** signed off **11 May 2023** and **applicable from 1 Sep 2023**. Read on the ASCI guidelines page (original).
- **Re-check by:** 2024-05-11, **OVERDUE**; unchanged on the live page.
- They apply Chapter I to digital ads, apps and sites, covering four patterns:
  1. **Drip pricing:** the displayed price must include unavoidable taxes and fees that most buyers pay.
  2. **Bait and switch:** this includes a price changing after selection, a promoted item turning out to be "out of stock" with a swap offered, or an "X" button that opens the thing instead of closing it.
  3. **False urgency:** saying or implying stock or availability is scarcer than it is. If a complaint is made, the advertiser must prove that stock levels at the time justified the message.
  4. **Disguised ads:** content that looks editorial or organic, such as influencer posts or paid reviews, must be clearly marked as an ad.

**Government: Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA), "Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023"**
- Notified **30 Nov 2023** under s.18 of the Consumer Protection Act 2019. **This is binding law**, unlike ASCI.
- It applies to platforms systematically offering goods or services in India, **advertisers** and sellers.
- **Re-check by:** 2024-11-30, **OVERDUE**; there are later developments, listed below.
- **What I read:** coverage only, not the Gazette text:
  - Legal500 / Tuli & Co, "CCPA's Guidelines On Dark Patterns: An Overview", 17 Sep 2026: https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/consumer-protection/ccpas-guidelines-on-dark-patterns-an-overview
  - Bar & Bench / ELP, "India's Evolving Framework for the Regulation of Dark Patterns", 1 Jul 2025: https://www.barandbench.com/law-firms/view-point/indias-evolving-framework-for-the-regulation-of-dark-patterns
- **The 13 specified patterns:**
  1. false urgency (fake scarcity or popularity);
  2. basket sneaking;
  3. confirm shaming;
  4. forced action;
  5. subscription trap;
  6. interface interference;
  7. bait and switch;
  8. drip pricing;
  9. disguised advertisement (ads posing as reviews or news);
  10. nagging;
  11. trick question;
  12. SaaS billing;
  13. rogue malware.
- **Later developments:**
  - CCPA self-audit advisory, 5–7 Jun 2025: platforms were to self-audit within 3 months, with optional self-declarations.
  - 11 notices issued for dark-pattern violations (as of mid-2025).
  - IRDAI directions to insurers (2 Apr and 25 May 2026).
  - RBI dark-pattern rules for regulated entities, announced 15 Jun 2026 and **effective 1 Jan 2027**.
  - SEBI consultation, 23 Jun 2026.
- **Relevance to small-business short video:** "only 2 left" or "offer ends tonight" overlays, and fake countdowns, must be true at the time they are shown.

### (g) "No.1" / superlative claims
- **There is no dedicated ASCI guideline on "No.1" or superlative claims** on either page (I searched the full text).
- The rules that apply are:
  - **Chapter I.1** (substantiation);
  - **I.2** (name the source and date of research);
  - **I.4** (no misleading exaggeration);
  - **I.6** (clearly humorous puffery is allowed);
  - **Chapter IV** (comparisons must be factual, like-for-like and not chosen to create an artificial advantage).
- **Awards/rankings guideline** (signed off **21 Apr 2022**; re-check by 2023-04-21, **OVERDUE**):
  - An award cannot stand in for research or testing as proof of superiority.
  - The ad must name the awarding body and the month and year.
  - An award on an annual cycle is valid for 12 months.
  - The awarding body's written consent is needed.
  - A local survey cannot be stretched into a national or global claim.
  - There must be no paid or commercial link with the awarder.
  - Survey-based awards need an MRSI- or ESOMAR-member agency, with the sample and method disclosed.
- **Disclaimers guideline:** a broad claim such as "best … in India" cannot be cut back by a small-print limitation.
- **Precedent (original ASCI document):** ASCI's June 2019 complaint decisions, press release dated 29 Aug 2019: https://www.ascionline.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/asci-june-2019-ccc-recom.pdf
  - Several "No.1" / "India's No.1" / "biggest" claims by news channels were upheld as misleading because they were unproven and named no source.
  - A detergent's "No.1 choice of millions" claim failed because it had no nationwide market-survey or third-party data.
  - "India's Favourite …" claims backed only by app-store star ratings were rejected.
  - In short, the expectation is that leadership claims need independent, representative, current data and a named source.
- **"New/improved" claims:** may be used for 1 year from launch and must say what is new (guideline signed off 1 Jun 2014).
